Retrieval beats rereading

Why pulling an idea out of your head teaches more than putting it back in, why it feels worse while it works better, and why this dashboard counts proof instead of time.

prereq: none · cheat sheet: study moves · about 15 minutes

Why this lesson exists. This topic is an example that ships with edu4self, so you can try the whole loop once: read, answer, submit, get graded. It's also short and true, and every lesson Claude writes for you later is built on the idea in it.

0. Before you read: one question, closed book

Don't look anything up. Two students study the same chapter for the same total time. One reads it four times. The other reads it once, then three times closes the book and writes down everything they remember. One week later, both take a test.

A. Who remembers more a week later?

The one who practised recalling. And if you guessed wrong, notice that you just did the thing this lesson is about: you had to produce an answer before being told. That attempt makes the explanation below stick better, even when the attempt is wrong.

1. Two kinds of strength

The psychologists Robert and Elizabeth Bjork describe every memory as having two separate strengths:

  • Retrieval strength: how easily you can bring it to mind right now.
  • Storage strength: how deeply it's learned, how well it will survive time.

Rereading raises retrieval strength. The page is in front of you, everything looks familiar, it feels like learning. But familiarity is not recall. Close the book a week later and most of it is gone, because storage strength barely moved.

The surprising part: storage strength grows most when retrieval is hard. Struggling to pull something out, and then getting it, or getting it wrong and being corrected, is what builds the long-lasting kind of memory. The Bjorks call this a desirable difficulty.

2. The evidence, briefly

In a well-known experiment (Roediger and Karpicke, 2006), students read short science passages. Some reread them; others read once and then took recall tests with no feedback. Five minutes later, the rereaders did slightly better. A week later the result flipped: the students who had practised recall remembered clearly more. The rereaders were also the more confident group.

A large review of study techniques (Dunlosky and colleagues, 2013) rated two methods as the most useful across ages and subjects: practice testing and spacing (spreading practice out over days instead of cramming). Highlighting and rereading came out near the bottom.

B. One word: rereading mostly raises which strength?

Retrieval strength: the in-the-moment kind. It feels great and fades fast.

3. What this means for how edu4self works

  1. Every lesson ends in questions you answer without looking. That's the retrieval.
  2. Probes test before teaching. Trying first, even when you fail, makes the teaching that follows land harder. "I don't know" is a real answer, and a useful one.
  3. Recall checks come back days later. That's spacing. A thing isn't "learned" until you can produce it cold after a gap.
  4. The evidence log only lights up for proof. Time spent goes on your die; demonstrated ability goes on the evidence log. They are different things, and the dashboard keeps them apart on purpose.

Expect it to feel harder than rereading. That's not a sign it's working worse. It's often the sign it's working.

C. You finish a lesson and it all felt easy and familiar. What does that tell you about whether you'll remember it next week?

Feeling fluent right now is a poor predictor of later recall. It isn't proof of failure either: it's just not evidence. The only way to know is to test yourself after a gap.

  2. Why might rereading make you feel more confident while helping you remember less?
